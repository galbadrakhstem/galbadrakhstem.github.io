  <h2>Personal statement</h2>
  <div>

<p>There is a specific kind of joy that has no perfect name.</p>

<p>It happens in a quiet classroom, mid-explanation, when you watch someone's expression shift — the slight widening of the eyes, the almost imperceptible pause, the moment a concept that was once a foreign language suddenly becomes their own. I have seen that expression many times now. And every single time, without exception, it stops me mid-sentence.</p>

<p>That is why I teach.</p>

<p>Astronomy changed the way I see the world.</p>

<p>Not gradually, not subtly — but in the way that learning one true thing about the universe makes everything around you look slightly different afterward. The sky is no longer just a sky. The ground beneath your feet is not just ground. It is ancient, assembled from the remnants of dying stars, sitting on a tilted rock orbiting an ordinary star in one of two hundred billion galaxies. Even every single atom in my body is created in the deep core of ancient stars, which means the very fundamental blocks that resemble me are older than the planet we are living on. Once you understand that — really understand it, not just read it — you cannot unsee it.</p>

<p>That shift in perspective is the most valuable thing astronomy ever gave me. And it is the reason I teach.</p>

<p>I was in fifth grade when astronomy first cracked my world open. My teacher mentioned, almost in passing, that Earth's axis is tilted 23.5 degrees — and that this single fact, this one quiet angle, is responsible for every winter, every summer, every season humanity has ever lived through. I sat with that for a long time. Such a small number. Such an enormous consequence. It made me wonder how many other invisible, elegant rules were quietly governing everything around me without my knowledge.</p>

<p>So I started looking.</p>

<p>Every fact I found led to three more questions. Stars were not just lights — they were engines, furnaces, clocks, and graveyards. Galaxies were not just beautiful — they were colliding, evolving, moving away from us at speeds proportional to their distance. The universe itself had a beginning, a measurable age, and a geometry that bent around mass. The more I learned, the more I felt something I did not yet have words for — a kind of grateful astonishment at being alive in a cosmos this strange and this knowable.</p>

<p>Astronomy did not just teach me facts. It taught me how to be amazed. The very odds of life ever arising in the first place are almost impossible to imagine.</p>

<p>But my journey was not easy, and I want to be honest about that.</p>

<p>When I decided to study astronomy seriously, I discovered very quickly that the resources I needed did not exist — not in Mongolia, not in my language, not at the level I required. Astronomy is absent from our national curriculum entirely. There are no structured textbooks for high school students, no established teachers who can guide a student systematically from curiosity to competence. I searched online for hours, days and months and found fragments — videos in foreign languages, problem sets without explanations, concepts introduced without the foundations needed to understand them.</p>

<p>There were nights I nearly stopped.</p>

<p>I did not stop. Instead, I found past papers from the Mongolian National Astronomy Olympiad and worked through them one by one. I reached out to Dr. Nyamsuren, Director of the Institute of Astronomy and Geophysics, and asked for archival materials. I sat beside my father through late evenings, both of us working through equations that neither of us fully understood yet — and then, slowly, understanding them. I watched YouTube videos on the same topic twenty times until something clicked. I built my knowledge the way you build something without instructions: carefully, imperfectly, with a great deal of patience. Those months taught me far more than facts: they taught me ideas that can change how a person sees the world. If you want something consistently, there is always a way forward, even when it feels impossible, and the universe seems to help you reach the level you aimed for.</p>

<p>That persistence led me to the 9th National Astronomy Olympiad in April 2025.</p>

<p>I won a Gold Medal.</p>

<p>It was one achievement. But it meant something larger to me than a single result. It proved that a student in Mongolia — without institutional support, without a teacher, without a curriculum — could build genuine expertise through determination alone. It was the first real step in a journey I now understood was just beginning.</p>

<p>And then came the thought I could not let go of:</p>

<p>If I had to go through all of that to get here — what about everyone else?</p>

<p>What about the students who were curious but less stubborn? The ones who searched for resources, found nothing, and quietly decided that astronomy was simply not for them? What about the younger version of me, sitting alone at his desk, needing someone to show him where to start?</p>

<p>That thought became a responsibility.</p>

<p>On November 14, 2024, I founded the Physics &amp; Astronomy Club at Mongol Aspiration International School. My purpose was not complicated: I wanted to give students the starting point I never had. I wanted to build a community where curiosity was welcomed, where questions had space to breathe, and where a student who knew nothing about astrophysics could grow — step by step, systematically — toward something real.</p>

<p>But I wanted something more specific than that.</p>

<p>I wanted them to see the world the way I now saw it.</p>

<p>Teaching, I discovered, is not simply the transfer of information. It is the deliberate recreation of a shift in perspective — and that is far harder, and far more meaningful, than it sounds.</p>

<p>I taught orbital mechanics to my club members throughout my 9th and 10th grade years — returning to the same material again and again, each time with new students, new questions, and new gaps to bridge. In my 10th grade year alone, I taught the subject at least ten complete times. And something unexpected happened with each repetition: I understood the topic more deeply than I had before.</p>

<p>Not because I was reviewing notes. But because every student who asked why — without accepting a surface answer — forced me to approach the concept from a new direction. I learned to notice when my explanation was technically correct but practically useless. I learned to replace complexity with clarity without sacrificing truth. I learned to find the simplest possible example that preserved the full weight of the idea.</p>

<p>I was mastering the subject by teaching it. And in doing so, I began to understand what teaching actually is.</p>

<p>It is not about filling a student with knowledge. It is about changing the angle from which they look at the world — and then stepping back to watch them see something they could not see before.</p>

<p>That moment, the slight widening of the eyes, the pause, the quiet oh, is the reason I teach. Every single time, without exception, it moves me.</p>

<p>In early July 2026, shortly after completing 10th grade, I was invited to teach the astronomy component to students preparing for the International Earth Science Olympiad. These were serious, motivated students. And yet the same gap was there — no systematic guide, no clear starting point, no teacher who could walk them through the subject with both rigor and accessibility.</p>

<p>I stood in front of that room and I taught them.</p>

<p>Afterward, I began writing.</p>

<p>I had already compiled extensive research notebooks during my volunteer placement at Khurel Togoot Observatory the previous summer. What those notebooks needed was shape — pedagogical structure, clarity, a student on the other end. In mid-February 2026, I began transforming them into a formal textbook: Cosmic Calculations: Orbital Mechanics — Cracking the Code of the Solar System, written specifically for Mongolian high school students.</p>

<p>When I distributed the first copies to my club members, their comprehension improved immediately. Students who had previously found the material distant and intimidating were working through orbital mechanics problems with quiet confidence.</p>

<p>I am still writing. The series continues — Telescopes &amp; Magnification, Observation &amp; Light Intensity, and Historical Figures of Astronomy are in development. Each volume is one more entry point, one more door left open for a student who might otherwise find the subject locked.</p>

<p>I never expected to grow this much in two years.</p>

<p>I have won a Gold Medal at the National Olympiad, a Gold Medal at the Open World Astronomy Olympiad — Mongolia's first ever in that competition — a Silver Medal at the IAAC where I ranked as Mongolia's 3rd best participant, and a Bronze Medal at the subsequent National Olympiad. I am preparing now for the International Olympiad on Astronomy and Astrophysics (IOAA).</p>

<p>But none of those results are the thing I am most proud of.</p>

<p>The thing I am most proud of is watching a student in my club look up from a problem set they could not solve twenty minutes ago — and solve it. Watching a member who had never heard of Kepler stand up and deliver a research presentation on orbital resonance. Watching six students from my school walk away from the National Olympiad with medals.</p>

<p>Watching someone see the universe differently than they did before.</p>

<p>That is what astronomy gave me. A new way of seeing — the understanding that this world, this life, this moment is embedded in something almost incomprehensibly vast and almost incomprehensibly beautiful, and that the rules governing all of it are discoverable, learnable, and worth spending a lifetime pursuing.</p>

<p>I want to give that to others.</p>

<p>Not the medals. Not the competition results. Not even the knowledge, exactly.</p>

<p>The perspective.</p>

<p>The feeling of standing under a night sky and knowing, really knowing, what you are looking at. The quiet, permanent shift that happens when the universe stops being a backdrop and starts being something you understand.</p>

<p>Once a student sees the world that way, they cannot unsee it.</p>

<p>And that, more than anything, is why I teach.</p>
