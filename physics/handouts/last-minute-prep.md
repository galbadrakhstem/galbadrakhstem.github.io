# Last-minute prep

<p class="lead">The last week is not the time to learn a new topic. It is the time to make sure the physics you already know survives the pressure of the examination hall.</p>

## The five-day plan

<div class="table-wrap">
<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Day</th><th>What to do</th><th>Why</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Five days before</td><td>Write out, from memory, the definitions and the equations you must recall, and check which are printed on the data and formulae page. Then check them against the syllabus.</td><td>You find the gaps while there is still time to close them.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Four days before</td><td>One full past paper under timed conditions. Mark it yourself with the official mark scheme.</td><td>Timing and mark allocation are examined as much as physics.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Three days before</td><td>Re-do every question you got wrong. Write down what the error actually was — unit, sign, precision or definition.</td><td>Most lost marks come from a small number of repeated slips.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Two days before</td><td>Practical skills: draw up one results table, plot one graph with a line of best fit, find its gradient, and combine the uncertainties.</td><td>Papers 3 and 5 reward the same conventions every time.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>The day before</td><td>One or two short questions, then stop. Check the start time, the duration, your calculator and the stationery you are allowed.</td><td>Arrive rested and clear about the practical details.</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
</div>

## Conditions people forget

<ul class="link-list">
  <li><strong>Units and prefixes:</strong> convert to SI before substituting — grams to kilograms, cm² to m² (a factor of 10⁻⁴), electronvolts to joules — and give a unit with every answer.</li>
  <li><strong>Significant figures:</strong> give the final answer to the number of significant figures in the least precise data, usually two or three; in a “show that” question, give one more figure than the value you are asked to show.</li>
  <li><strong>Uncertainties:</strong> add absolute uncertainties when quantities are added or subtracted, add percentage uncertainties when they are multiplied or divided, and multiply the percentage uncertainty by n for a quantity raised to the power n.</li>
  <li><strong>Vectors and signs:</strong> give the direction of a vector answer and keep one sign convention through a whole question — displacement, velocity, momentum and field strength all have direction.</li>
  <li><strong>Kirchhoff’s laws:</strong> the first law is a consequence of conservation of charge and the second of conservation of energy; mark schemes look for those words.</li>
  <li><strong>Kelvin and radians:</strong> gas laws and thermodynamics use thermodynamic temperature, T / K = θ / °C + 273.15; circular motion, simple harmonic motion and alternating currents use radians.</li>
  <li><strong>Potential at infinity:</strong> gravitational and electric potential are zero at infinity, so gravitational potential is always negative.</li>
  <li><strong>Paper 3 tables and graphs:</strong> every column heading gives a quantity and its unit, raw readings in a column share the precision of the instrument, and the plotted points should cover at least half the grid.</li>
</ul>
